Sky Lagoon: prices, opening hours and what time to book

Thirteen minutes from the centre, seven steps, one horizon. The package you choose matters less than the hour you book.

By Eugene · Updated 6 October 2026
What time to book Lagoon tickets & transfers

Sky Lagoon is an infinity-edge geothermal lagoon on the sea, about 13 minutes from the centre of Reykjavik. Two passes, one ritual, water at 38–40 °C and an ocean horizon instead of a lava field.

For a short trip it sits well at the end of a day: leave the daylight to central Reykjavik and come out to the water once the walking is done. If you want that first half ready-made, our TouringBee audio walk runs about 2–2.5 hours and works entirely offline.

Most pages about Sky Lagoon spend their length comparing Saman with Sér. That is the smaller of the two decisions. The bigger one is the hour on your ticket: the Skjól ritual — the seven-step sequence you are paying for — closes an hour before the lagoon does. The bar closes with it, the café half an hour earlier, and guests are asked out of the water 30 minutes before the doors shut. Book the last slot and you still get the ritual; what you do not get is any slack around it.

Then there is the horizon. The sunset people come here for is at about half past three around the winter solstice and nearly at midnight in June. "The sunset slot" is a different hour in every month of the year. And one rule removes the plan entirely for some travellers: children under 12 are not admitted.

Book backwards from closing time

Here is the sequence the operator publishes, and almost nobody reads before buying. Taking winter hours, when the lagoon runs 10:00 to 21:00:

TimeWhat happens
18:30Last entry slot sold — two and a half hours before closing
20:00Skjól ritual closes. So does Gelmir Bar, the one in the lagoon
20:30Keimur Café closes
20:30Guests asked to leave the water
21:00Doors close

Read it from the bottom and the shape is clear. An 18:30 entry leaves ninety minutes before the ritual closes. That is enough to undress, shower, get into the water and go through all seven steps — but undressing, showering and any queue come out of the same ninety minutes, so there is nothing spare. If you want the ritual without watching the clock, and an hour in the water afterwards, go earlier.

In winter I would aim at 16:00 to 17:00. That is close to sunset, which is the point of coming, and it still leaves three to four hours before the doors close. The same rule in any season: count back three and a half to four hours from closing, then check it against the sunset time for your date.

Slots are half-hourly, and the first one is always opening time. What changes with the season is the last one — the booking calendar simply stops selling two and a half hours before the doors close.

What you actually pay

Two passes. Both include lagoon access, one journey through the Skjól ritual, unlimited time in the water and the cold plunge, towels, and a wristband that is your locker key and your bar tab in one. Saman uses public changing rooms with shower cubicles and a shared vanity area. Sér adds a private changing room and shower, a private vanity with a hairdryer, more generous toiletries and a reusable waterproof bag.

Which one? For most people, Saman. The lagoon, the ritual, the cold plunge and the towel are identical in both. Sér buys you a private room to change and shower in, a waterproof bag and better toiletries. If changing in a shared room is something you would rather not do, it is worth the roughly 3,000 ISK. If it is not, that money does nothing for your evening.

And treat the headline figure as a floor, not a price. Checking the live calendar on 6 October 2026, the same-day Saman slots were 14,990 ISK and every slot on 17, 18 and 25 October was 16,990 ISK. The hour did not change the number on any of those days — the date did. If your trip is flexible by a day, it is worth clicking through two of them before you book.

Slots also sell out. On that same check, three morning slots on a date nineteen days ahead were already gone. Free cancellation runs up to 24 hours before, so an early booking costs you nothing but the decision.

The seven steps, and one allergy note

The Skjól ritual runs in a fixed order and you go through it once. The lagoon itself at 38–40 °C, then a cold plunge at around 5 °C, then a turf-walled room. After that a sauna at 80–90 °C with a window on the sea, a cold mist, a body scrub, and a steam room to finish. You can stay in the water as long as you like before and after; it is the ritual circuit that is once-through.

Worth knowing if anyone in your group has a nut or seed allergy: the Mýkt body scrub contains almond oil and sesame seed oil. Using the scrub is optional.

Each adult may buy up to three alcoholic drinks during a visit, paid for with the wristband, and entry is refused if you turn up drunk. In water at 38–40 °C, three is plenty.

How to get to Sky Lagoon from central Reykjavik

First, the thing that catches people out: Sky Lagoon is not in Reykjavik. It is in Kópavogur, the municipality that starts a few streets south of the city, out on the Kársnes peninsula at Vesturvör 44–48. Give a driver the address rather than the city.

By car it is about 13 minutes from the centre, 12 from Hallgrímskirkja, 9 from the BSÍ bus terminal, 14 from Harpa and 45 from Keflavík airport. Parking is free, which in this city is not nothing.

Without a car, take bus 4 from Hlemmur to Hamraborg, about 15 minutes, then bus 35 or 36 to Bakkabraut, about five more, then walk. The operator also sells a shuttle transfer as an add-on at booking. For two or more people a taxi from the old centre is short enough to be worth pricing before you assume the bus is better value.

For a short trip to Reykjavik I would put Sky Lagoon at the end of a day. One and a half to two hours inside plus travel both ways is roughly three hours, and those are hours the city uses better in daylight than the lagoon does. Mornings work perfectly well if that suits you better — this is a preference, not a rule.

The horizon you are paying for

The selling point is the infinity edge against the open sea, and what makes it worth the trip is what is happening on that horizon while you are in the water. That moves more here than almost anywhere people travel to.

Around the winter solstice the sun sets in Reykjavik at about half past three in the afternoon, after rising at around twenty past eleven. A 15:00 slot in December is the sunset slot. In June the sun barely sets at all and the same experience is at eleven at night. Look up the sunset time for your actual date before you choose the hour — it is the one piece of homework this page cannot do for you.

The other thing people hope for is the aurora, and honesty is worth more here than optimism. It needs darkness, a clear sky and solar activity, none of which a booking can arrange, and the lagoon's own lighting works against you. It happens. It is not a reason to pick a slot.

Three visits

If you have one evening

Walk the old centre while it is light, eat early, and take a slot three to four hours before closing. You get the ritual without watching the clock and an hour in the water afterwards with the lights of the bay on one side and nothing at all on the other.

If you are landing or leaving the same day

This is possible, but leave a large margin. Sky Lagoon is about 45 minutes from Keflavík by car in normal conditions, and weather and road conditions in Iceland change that without warning. Large luggage goes into storage at reception for 990 ISK. Check the day's last slot before you plan around it — in winter that is 18:30 — and if the flight is tight, this is the thing to drop rather than the thing to rush.

If you are travelling with children

Under 12s cannot come in, so Sky Lagoon is a plan for an evening when someone else has the children, or not a plan at all. Reykjavik's municipal geothermal pools take all ages for a fraction of the price and are where the city actually swims. For 12 to 14-year-olds, a guardian aged 18 or over has to be in the group, and staff may ask for ID.

Five mistakes

  1. Taking the last slot if you want an unhurried visit. There is technically time for the ritual, but almost no margin: changing, showering and any queue come out of the same ninety minutes.
  2. Choosing the package before the date. Both move the price — Saman to Sér was about 3,000 ISK, and the same Saman pass was 14,990 or 16,990 ISK depending on the day. Check both before you book either.
  3. Assuming it is in Reykjavik. It is in Kópavogur, and the street address will get you there faster than the city name.
  4. Planning it around children. Under 12s are not admitted. Find this out now rather than at reception.
  5. Treating the sunset as fixed. It is at about 15:30 in December and near midnight in June. Look up your date.

Common questions

How much does Sky Lagoon cost?

The Saman pass starts at 14,990 ISK for an adult and 7,495 ISK for a 12 to 14-year-old; the Sér pass, which adds private changing rooms, starts at 17,990 ISK and 8,995 ISK. The price depends on the date as well as the pass: in our check on 6 October 2026 the same Saman pass cost 14,990 or 16,990 ISK depending on the day.

Can children go to Sky Lagoon?

Not under 12. The operator states that children under the age of 12 are not permitted, and that 12 to 14-year-olds must be accompanied by a guardian aged 18 or over. Staff may ask for ID. If you are travelling with younger children, this is the one Reykjavik spa to plan around rather than into.

What time should I book Sky Lagoon?

The last entry is sold two and a half hours before closing, which is enough for the ritual but leaves little slack. For an unhurried visit, aim to arrive about three and a half to four hours before closing — in winter, when the lagoon shuts at 21:00, that means somewhere around 16:00 to 17:00.

Is Sky Lagoon in Reykjavik?

No, it is in Kópavogur, the municipality immediately south of the city, at Vesturvör 44–48 on the Kársnes peninsula. In practice that means about 13 minutes by car from the centre and free parking when you get there — closer than the airport road and much closer than the Blue Lagoon.

How do I get to Sky Lagoon without a car?

Bus 4 from Hlemmur to Hamraborg takes about 15 minutes, then bus 35 or 36 to Bakkabraut adds about five, and a short walk finishes it. The operator also sells a shuttle transfer as an add-on when you book. A taxi from the old centre is a short enough ride that it is worth pricing for two or more people.

How long do you need at Sky Lagoon?

The operator suggests one and a half to two hours, and that is about right for one pass through the seven-step ritual with time left to sit in the water afterwards. Add travel each way and the visit takes roughly three hours out of your day.

The short version

Take Saman unless a private changing room is worth about 3,000 ISK to you, and spend the attention you save on the hour instead. Three to four hours before closing gives you the ritual unhurried and the water on both sides of it; in winter that is around 16:00 to 17:00. Check your date before the package, and the sunset before either.
