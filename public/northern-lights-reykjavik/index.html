Northern lights in Reykjavik: when the sky is dark enough, and where to stand

You cannot book the aurora. You can read the cloud map the right way round, and know that the sky keeps getting darker for two hours after the forecast says it is dark.

By Eugene · Updated 16 October 2026
How dark it needs to be Aurora tours from Reykjavik

Nobody can promise you the northern lights, and any page that does is selling something. Three things have to line up: a dark enough sky, a clear one and solar activity. Only the first is knowable in advance — and most visitors misread even that, because the forecast they are using does not mean what it looks like it means.

The aurora takes your nights and gives nothing back on the ones it skips. The days are the part you can actually plan, and around the winter solstice Reykjavik has barely four hours of daylight. Our TouringBee audio walk runs 2–2.5 hours and fits inside even that.

So this page is about the two decisions that are yours: when to go out, and where to stand. The rest is weather.

The forecast says "Dark" while the sky is still getting darker

The Icelandic Met Office publishes an aurora page with a cloud map, an activity number and, underneath, a line for Reykjavik that reads something like Dark: 18:54 to 07:35. It is the most useful number on the internet for this, and it is routinely read as "the sky is now black". It is not.

That line marks the end of civil twilight — the sun 6° below the horizon. The sky goes on deepening for about two more hours after it, through nautical twilight and into astronomical night, when the sun is more than 18° down and the sky stops glowing at all.

None of which means you have to wait. A bright aurora is perfectly visible in twilight, and on a strong night people see it not long after sunset. What the deepening buys you is the faint stuff: every hour of twilight that passes makes a weak, grey arc easier to pick out from the sky behind it. So the rule is not "wait for astronomical night" — it is "if nothing is showing yet, the sky has not finished, and the same aurora will be easier to see later."

That gap holds at roughly two hours right through the season, and it does not shrink in midwinter — on 15 December it is two hours and eight minutes, slightly longer than October's. Whatever month you come in, the forecast's "Dark" is the start of the useful window, not the middle of it.

The season, and what the dark months actually buy you

The usual answer is "late August to mid-April", and as a season that is about right. The aurora is visible whenever the sky is dark enough and the sun is active, which in practice means the dark half of the year.

There is a sharper number underneath it, though. Reykjavik only gets true astronomical night — sun more than 18° down — between roughly 2 September and 8 April. That is not the boundary of the season; a strong aurora in late August will show in twilight perfectly well. It is the boundary of the darkest skies, which is what a faint display needs.

These are mid-month figures for Reykjavik and they move by a few minutes a day. Look up your own date before planning around them.

Green means clouds

The Met Office map shows forecast cloud cover. White areas are clear sky. Green areas are cloudy. On a page about green lights in the sky, this catches people out constantly, and it matters more than anything else on the screen.

Underneath it sits the activity forecast, a number from 0 to 9 for midnight. It is worth a glance, but it is rarely the thing stopping you. Iceland sits under the auroral oval, which is why the lights appear here on quiet nights when southern Europe sees nothing. And however strong a display is, solid cloud hides it — so read the cloud first and the number second. Cloud is patchy, though, and forecasts are approximate: a bright aurora can find a gap.

So read the page in this order: white first, then the clock, then the number. If the whole south-west is green, the question is not whether the aurora is strong — it is whether you are willing to drive to where the map is white.

Where to stand without leaving the city

You do not need a tour to try. You need to get away from streetlights and have open sky, and Reykjavik is small enough that this is a short trip rather than an expedition.

Grótta, on the Seltjarnarnes peninsula, is one of the best spots you can reach from the city without a tour. It is about 10–15 minutes by car from the centre — longer by bus and a walk, and a long walk on its own — and the view out to sea is unobstructed. There is a small geothermal foot bath, Kvika, at about 39 °C for the wait. Two honest caveats. It is popular, so expect other people, car headlights and some glow from the nearby streets. And the causeway out to the lighthouse island is tidal, floods quickly, and people have been cut off on it. Watch from the shore, which is where the view is anyway.

Closer in, the north-facing waterfront is a reasonable fallback on a strong night: the sea is on one side and the city's lights are behind you. It is the same stretch the walking route follows in daylight, which makes it the easiest darker spot to find when you are new here. It will not show you a faint display — for that you need to leave the lit streets properly.

Wherever you end up, the discipline is the same. North is the usual direction to face, but do not fix on it: when activity is high the aurora can spread across much of the sky, including straight overhead. Give your eyes twenty minutes to adjust, and if you have to check the forecast, drop the screen brightness or use your phone's red or night mode rather than a full-brightness map. To the naked eye a weak aurora is a pale grey arc that most people take for cloud — a camera will render it green long before you are sure you can see anything at all.

What a tour actually buys

Mostly, mobility under a cloud map. On a night when the capital is overcast and somewhere ninety minutes away is clear, a bus can be there and you cannot. A good operator adds more than the driving. They read the local weather as the evening goes, change the route when the forecast shifts, know which pull-offs are safe to stand at in the dark, and help you get a usable photograph. None of that guarantees an aurora, and anyone who says otherwise is overselling.

If you are comparing two tours that look identical, the clause worth reading is the second attempt: many operators let you come again free if the lights do not show, and the terms vary. That, not the photo on the listing, is the difference between them.

And the honest counter-argument: if you are in Reykjavik for several winter nights, you can simply look out every night for free, and your odds across four nights are better than any single tour's. Buy the tour when your nights are few, or when the city is socked in and the map says the next valley is not.

Three nights

If the map is white and the number is low

Still worth going out. Iceland sits far enough north that modest activity under a clear sky can show something, though plenty of such nights come to nothing. Grótta, twenty minutes of adjusting, half an hour of patience — it costs you an evening you were spending here anyway.

If the map is green over the whole south-west

This is the night to buy mobility or to go to bed. Standing in the cloud will not improve it, and a tour that drives two hours to a clear valley is doing the only thing that can be done.

If you have one night and it is September

Check the activity forecast before you decide when to go out. If it points to a strong evening and the sky is clear, go as soon as the sky has lost its glare — a bright display does not wait for midnight. If activity looks modest, the sky is at its darkest after about eleven, so eat late and use the second half of the evening.

Six mistakes

  1. Giving up at the forecast's "Dark". That is the end of civil twilight, not the end of the night. If nothing is showing, the sky has two more hours of deepening to do.
  2. Reading green as good. On the Met Office map green is cloud. White is the colour you want.
  3. Reading the activity number before the cloud map. Solid cloud hides any display, however strong. Clear sky first, number second.
  4. Checking the forecast on a full-brightness screen. Twenty minutes of adjusting, set back by one bright map. Dim it, or use night mode.
  5. Walking out to the Grótta lighthouse in the dark. The causeway is tidal and floods fast. The shore has the same view and stays where it is.
  6. Staring only at the north. It is the usual direction, but a strong display can cover much of the sky, overhead included.

Common questions

When can you see the northern lights in Reykjavik?

Roughly from late August to mid-April, whenever the sky gets dark enough and the sun is active. The darkest skies come in a narrower window: Reykjavik only gets true astronomical night — the sun more than 18° below the horizon — from about 2 September to about 8 April. Outside that, a bright display still shows against twilight; a faint one is much harder.

What does the “Dark” time on the Icelandic forecast mean?

It is the end of civil twilight. On 16 October 2026 the Met Office listed darkness from 18:54, but the sun did not reach 18° below the horizon until 20:47, nearly two hours later. You do not have to wait that long, because a bright aurora shows in twilight. But if the sky looks empty at the first time, it is worth staying out: the same display gets easier to see as the evening goes on.

Can you see the northern lights from inside Reykjavik?

Yes, on a strong night, and people do. City light raises the bar: a faint aurora that would show clearly at Grótta can be invisible from a lit street. The usual move is to walk fifteen minutes to the edge of the city, face north, and let your eyes adjust for twenty minutes before deciding the sky is empty.

Is green on the aurora map good or bad?

Bad. On the Icelandic Met Office map green means cloud and white means clear sky, which is the opposite of what almost everyone assumes on a page about green lights. Read the map for white first, and only then look at the activity number — though cloud is patchy and a bright display can still find a gap.

Are northern lights tours worth it?

Their main advantage is mobility: the driver can move all evening to wherever the cloud map is white, which is often the binding constraint. A good guide also reads the local weather, picks safe places to stop and helps with photographs. None of it guarantees a sighting. Before choosing between two similar tours, check whether a second attempt is free if the lights do not appear — many operators offer it, not all.

How long should I wait outside?

Give it at least half an hour, and let your eyes adjust for twenty minutes of that. The aurora is often faint and grey to the naked eye before it brightens. If you need to check the forecast, dim the screen or use night mode rather than a full-brightness map.

The short version

Come in the dark half of the year, and if you want the best odds on a faint display, come between September and early April when the sky reaches full darkness. Read the cloud map for white, not green. Treat the forecast's "Dark" as the start of the window rather than the end of it: a bright aurora shows in twilight, a faint one gets easier each hour after. Stand at Grótta, off the causeway, and give your eyes twenty minutes.
