Blue Lagoon: what it really costs, and which day of your trip to spend on it

It is 20 minutes from the airport and 60 minutes from Reykjavik. That one fact decides almost everything else — the price you pay, the bus you can catch, and whether it costs you a day in the city or nothing at all.

By Eugene · Updated 22 October 2026
Which day to go Compare lagoon tickets

The Blue Lagoon is not in Reykjavik and it is not near Reykjavik. It sits outside Grindavík, on the road between the capital and Keflavík airport — 20 minutes from the terminal, 60 minutes from the city. Both numbers come from the lagoon's own booking engine, and together they are the whole argument of this page.

Because of where it is, the real question is not whether to go. It is which day. Pinned to your arrival or your departure it costs you almost nothing in time. Taken out of the middle of a city trip it costs you a half day, two hours of bus and a 06:30 alarm.

We publish a Reykjavik audio walk, so we have an interest in how many of your hours the lagoon takes. That is exactly why this page counts them out loud, with the timetables attached, rather than telling you it is overrated.

What it actually costs

The price page says Comfort from €88. We opened the booking engine on 21 October 2026 and priced the same Comfort ticket on six dates spread across the year. It was never €88.

Two patterns survive that table, and one does not.

What holds. On all six dates the most expensive hour was 16:00 or 17:00 — the late-afternoon slot, which is also the one every itinerary recommends. And the swing within a single day ran from €23 to €34, which is more than the step from Comfort up to Premium: on 24 October the engine offered us that upgrade for €23 a head.

What does not. "Go in the morning, it is cheaper" is not true year-round. The 08:00 slot was at or near the day's floor on five of the six dates — but on 14 July it cost €123 while the 20:00 and 21:00 slots cost €111. In high summer the cheap hours move to the late evening, which in July is still broad daylight.

One more thing the table hides: it fills up. On that same Saturday in October, 11:00, 15:00 and 16:00 were already unavailable and 12:00 was showing six places left. "Pre-booking is essential" is not marketing copy here.

The three buses that decide your day

Here is the part that almost no guide prints. The lagoon's own transport partner runs plenty of buses to and from Reykjavik. To and from the airport, it runs very few.

RouteDeparturesTime
Keflavík airport → lagoon07:30, 09:30, 12:30, 16:3020 min
Lagoon → Keflavík airport12:00, 14:30, 16:0020 min
Reykjavik → lagoonHourly 07:00–18:00 from 21 Aug to 31 Jan; hourly 07:00–16:00 Feb–Mar; five a day Apr–May60 min
Lagoon → ReykjavikHourly 12:15–22:15 from 21 Aug to 31 Jan; hourly 12:15–20:15 Feb–Mar60 min

Three departures to the airport, the last at 16:00. We checked this inside the booking flow rather than taking it from a timetable page: with a morning admission selected, the drop-off list offered 12:00, 14:30 and 16:00 and nothing else.

One caveat on that table. The operator's published schedule still groups January with the autumn season, which would mean buses back to the city until 22:15. The lagoon's own hours close the doors at 20:00 from 1 January, and when we priced 19 January the engine agreed. Book the bus through the lagoon's checkout, which only offers departures that exist on your date.

Put that beside the price table and something useful falls out. The cheapest evening slots have no bus to the airport at all — the 18:00, 19:00 and 20:00 entries are stranded if the airport is where you are going next. But the morning slot, which is also at or near the day's floor for most of the year, connects to all three.

So on departure day the cheap choice and the possible choice are the same choice. That is a rare piece of luck and it is worth taking.

Going the other way, from the city, the arithmetic is less kind. For an 08:00 slot the engine recommends the 07:00 bus from Reykjavik, and prints, in its own words, be ready at 06:30 — you must be at your assigned stop half an hour before it leaves. Note "assigned stop": central Reykjavik hotels are not door pickups. You are sent to one of five points — Lækjargata, Harpa, Safnahúsið, Austurbær or the BSÍ terminal — and you stand there in the dark.

Which day to go

The operator's own booking flow assumes you will spend three hours at the lagoon. Take that as the unit and the three options look like this.

Departure day is the best version, as long as your flight is late enough. The 12:00 bus puts you at the terminal around 12:20 and the 14:30 around 14:50, so anything leaving from mid-afternoon onwards works comfortably. Book an 08:00 or 09:00 slot, store the bags, take the 12:00 or 14:30, and you have converted dead hotel-checkout time into the thing you came for.

Arrival day is the next best, and the kinder one if you land tired. The lagoon is 20 minutes from the door of the terminal, and from 21 August to 31 January a bus runs onward to Reykjavik every hour until 22:15. The catch is the airport departures — four a day — so your landing has to line up with 07:30, 09:30, 12:30 or 16:30.

A dedicated day from Reykjavik is the version to question. Two hours on a bus, half an hour at a bus stop, three hours in the water: that is most of a short day. And it is the day you are not spending in the city. If you have four nights it is fine. If you have two, it is expensive in a currency that is not krónur.

What you get for the money

Roughly 10,000 square metres of mineral-rich seawater at 37–40 °C, a little over a metre deep — the lagoon map says under 1.3 m, the help centre 1.4 m. Around it: a sauna with a view, a steam room, a steam cave cut into the rock, a waterfall you stand under. Also an in-water bar, a mask bar, and a quiet zone where phones are not allowed. Storytelling sessions run twice a day. It is bigger and stranger than the photographs suggest, and the water really is that colour.

What it is not is a secret. On a busy afternoon in the high season this is a major international attraction operating at capacity, and it feels like one. The morning and late-evening slots are quieter as well as cheaper, which is the same advice from two directions.

The add-ons, since the booking flow pushes them hard: a group float session is €45, a breakfast box €23, a robe or slippers from €15, an in-water massage from €160 and float therapy from €199. Lava Restaurant does a two-course meal bundled with Premium admission for €199–222 all in. None of it is necessary and all of it is good; just decide before the checkout page rather than during it.

Children, luggage and the small print

The minimum age is two — the help centre says younger children's bodies are too sensitive to the mineral content, and there is no option to book them. Ages 2 to 11 pay €15, listed as 2,000 ISK. Children aged 8 and under must wear floats, handed out free at the entrance, and one adult may supervise no more than two children. On the bus, children 11 and under travel free, though the transfer operator's own widget uses different age bands from the lagoon — worth a glance if your child is 12 or 13.

Lockers are included and take valuables and one backpack-sized bag. Anything larger goes to the storage by the car park at 1,000 ISK per bag, which is what makes the arrival and departure day plans work at all.

Two scheduling traps. The last bookable slot is two hours before closing, and you are asked to leave the water 30 minutes before the doors shut. The last entry of the day therefore buys you 90 minutes, not the three hours the transfer flow assumes. The second trap is that the winter hours change on 1 January, not 1 February: the lagoon's own site puts 08:00–20:00 from 1 January to 31 May, while the transfer partner's schedule page still shows the later closing through January. When we priced 19 January the engine gave 08:00–20:00 with a last slot of 18:00, so the lagoon's own hours are the ones to trust. Book the bus through the lagoon's checkout and it will only offer you times that exist on your date.

The volcano question

It deserves a straight answer rather than either silence or alarm. Between December 2023 and August 2025 the Sundhnúkur crater row, a few kilometres from the lagoon, erupted nine times, and the lagoon closed at short notice during that series. Checking on 21 October 2026, the Icelandic Met Office showed no alerts and normal conditions.

Nothing about that is a reason not to book. It is a reason to check the status on the morning of your visit, and to keep the lagoon off the critical path of your last day if you can. It is also a reason to drive road 43 rather than trust the satnav: the lagoon warns on its own site that some navigation apps still show outdated routes.

Blue Lagoon or Sky Lagoon

Both are geothermal, both are engineered, and the thing that separates them is not what most people assume.

Note what that table does not say. The Blue Lagoon is not the expensive one. Its cheap morning slots undercut Sky Lagoon's standard pass, and only its late-afternoon slots cost meaningfully more. On the dates we checked, moving from the cheapest to the dearest hour of a single day at the Blue Lagoon cost about as much as the entire list-price gap between the two lagoons.

So the choice is about hours, not krónur. If you have the days — and above all if you can pin it to a flight — go to the Blue Lagoon, because the landscape out there is the one people mean when they say Iceland. If your trip is short and every afternoon is spoken for, Sky Lagoon answers the same craving from inside the city.

Six mistakes

  1. Booking 16:00 or 17:00. It was the most expensive hour on every date we checked, and it is the busiest. Moving to 08:00 saved €23 to €34 a head.
  2. Planning a departure-day visit around an evening slot. The last bus to the airport leaves at 16:00. Take the morning.
  3. Budgeting €88. The cheapest we were offered was €92, and with the official bus a Reykjavik trip lands between about €159 and €182 a head.
  4. Leaving it to the day. Slots sell out: on one October Saturday three hours of the day were already gone.
  5. Taking the last slot for a long soak. Entry closes two hours before the doors do, and you leave the water 30 minutes before that.
  6. Trusting the satnav. Drive Grindavíkurvegur, road 43, and follow the signs — the lagoon says some apps still route people the old way.

Common questions

How much does the Blue Lagoon really cost?

The site advertises Comfort admission from €88. On six dates we checked in the lagoon's own booking engine the cheapest slot offered was €92 and the dearest €138, for exactly the same ticket. Add the official round-trip bus at €67 a head: the two-adult cart we actually priced for a Saturday came to €364.56, or €182 each, and a cheap weekday would be nearer €159. The ISK list price, 11,990 ISK, tracks the real floor better than the euro one does.

What time of day is cheapest at the Blue Lagoon?

On all six dates we sampled, 16:00 or 17:00 was the most expensive hour of the day. The 08:00 slot sat at or near the cheapest on five of the six — the exception was mid-July, where the floor moved to the 20:00 and 21:00 slots instead. The gap between the cheapest and dearest hour on one date ran from €23 to €34, which is more than the step up to the next admission tier.

How do you get to the Blue Lagoon from Keflavik airport?

The lagoon's transport partner runs four departures a day from the airport, year-round: 07:30, 09:30, 12:30 and 16:30. The drive is 20 minutes. From Reykjavik it is 60 minutes, with hourly departures through most of the year, and you need to be at your assigned city bus stop 30 minutes before the bus leaves. Rental cars and other operators also run; road 43, Grindavíkurvegur, is the access road.

Can you visit the Blue Lagoon on the way to your flight?

Yes, and it is the best use of it — but only three buses a day go from the lagoon to the airport: 12:00, 14:30 and 16:00. That makes a morning slot the one that works, which happens to be among the cheapest hours too. The cheap evening slots have no airport bus at all. Large luggage is stored by the car park at 1,000 ISK a bag; lockers inside take a backpack.

Is the Blue Lagoon better than Sky Lagoon?

Not on price, which is where most comparisons start. Comfort entry at the Blue Lagoon is listed at 11,990 ISK against Sky Lagoon's 14,990 ISK, and the gap between the Blue Lagoon's own cheapest and dearest hour is about as wide as the gap between the two lagoons. The difference is time. Sky Lagoon is a short ride from the centre and fits inside an evening; the Blue Lagoon is an hour out on the airport road and takes a half day unless you pin it to a flight.

Does the Blue Lagoon close because of the volcano?

It has. Between December 2023 and August 2025 the Sundhnúkur crater row erupted nine times a few kilometres away, and the lagoon has closed at short notice during that series. On 21 October 2026 the Icelandic Met Office showed no alerts and normal conditions. The practical rule has not changed: check the morning of your visit, and do not make it the only thing holding up your last day.

The short version

Go, but go on a flight day. The lagoon is 20 minutes from the terminal and an hour from the city, so pinning it to your arrival or departure costs you nothing and taking a special trip costs you half of one.

Book a morning slot. It is among the cheapest hours for most of the year, it is the quiet end of the day, and on departure day it is the only one that reaches a bus to the airport. Then budget honestly — somewhere between €159 and €182 a head once the bus is in, not the €88 on the price page.
