Best things to do in Reykjavik, ranked by what the daylight allows

Not a list of thirty sights. A list of what has to happen while the sun is up, what is just as good in the dark, and what each of them costs.

By Eugene · Updated 12 November 2026
Jump to the list Compare attraction tickets

Every list of things to do in Reykjavik is ranked by the wrong thing. Ranked by rating, you get the lagoons at the top and the church somewhere near ten. Ranked by what a real day here actually allows, the order changes completely — because in December this city gives you about four usable hours of light, and in June it gives you all of them.

So that is the ranking below. Not what is best, but what the sun lets you do. The daylight list is short and mostly free, and it is almost entirely the walk through the centre — the same line the self-guided walking tour follows. The after-dark list is long, warm and mostly indoors, and it is where the money goes.

The one number that decides your list

Reykjavik sits just under the Arctic Circle, and the swing between midsummer and midwinter is larger than almost anywhere most visitors have planned a trip before. In late December the sun clears the rooftops around 11:20 and is gone again by half past three. In June it barely sets.

That is not a weather detail. It is the structure of your day. Four hours of light means one outdoor block, not two — and everything you chose to spend it on is a thing you did not spend it on elsewhere. In June the constraint vanishes and the ranking below stops mattering, which is its own kind of luxury.

The second limit is a clock, not a season. Hallgrímskirkja is the only high viewpoint in the centre, and from 1 September to 31 May the church closes at 17:00 with the last lift at 16:45. Tickets are sold on the door and cannot be reserved. Miss it and there is no second chance that day.

Spend the daylight on these

Five things, and four of them are free. If you only get one bright block, this is what it is for.

  1. The walk through the old centre. Arnarhóll, Austurvöllur, the pond, the painted street, the church, the waterfront, the Old Harbour. About 5 km, flat except for one climb, two hours at a normal pace. Free. This is the thing people come for, even when they describe it as something else.
  2. The Hallgrímskirkja tower. 1,500 ISK for adults, 200 ISK for children aged 7 to 16, 1,300 ISK for students. The only view down onto the coloured roofs, and the one item on this page with a deadline. Going in is free; it is the lift that costs.
  3. The waterfront from Harpa to Grandi. The Sun Voyager, the glass concert hall, the Old Harbour with its boats and its grey coastguard ship, then Grandi and the grass mound at the end. Free, and pointless in the dark — this stretch is all sightlines across the bay to Mount Esja.
  4. The Einar Jónsson sculpture garden. Behind the museum next to the church: 26 bronze casts in a walled garden that is open to everyone, year-round, day and night, at no charge. It is the single most undersold free thing in the city centre.
  5. A boat, if the season is right. Whale watching runs year-round, but in midwinter the main operators drop to a single 13:00 departure — the middle of the light — and the trip runs two and a half to three and a half hours. Worth it, as long as you know it is the whole block.

Everything in that list except the tower is free, which is the part most people get wrong about Reykjavik. The expensive city is the eating and the day trips, not the sights.

The pools deserve singling out. A single adult entry is 1,500 ISK, children aged 10 to 17 pay 400 ISK and under-10s go free, and Laugardalslaug and Vesturbæjarlaug are open from 06:30 to 22:00 on weekdays. The only thing to book before you arrive

Sky Lagoon and the Blue Lagoon both run on timed entry, and the good evening slots go well ahead. Nothing else on this page needs a reservation — the museums, the pools and the tower all sell on the door.

Grandi: the half day most lists bury

Grandi is the former fish-processing quarter at the far end of the harbour, and it has quietly become where the indoor attractions cluster. If the weather turns, this is where to turn with it.

  • Whales of Iceland — full-size models of the whales in Icelandic waters, 4,300 ISK, daily 10:00–17:00 all year, with guided tours at 11:00 and 15:00.
  • FlyOver Iceland — 5,690 ISK online against 6,250 ISK at the door, children 3,690 ISK. The flight is eight to ten minutes; allow about an hour for the whole thing.
  • Reykjavík Maritime Museum — 2,550 ISK, daily 10:00–17:00, in an old fish factory on the quay.
  • ICGV Óðinn — the coastguard vessel moored outside the maritime museum, with a combat record against a far larger navy. Guided tours aboard run from 1 March to 31 October and take a little under an hour. A combined museum and ship ticket is 3,850 ISK.
  • Þúfa — a spiral path up an artificial grass mound with a fish-drying shed on top. Free, and the quietest view in the city.

Grandi sits at the far end of the same waterfront line, a walk from Harpa along the water, and the walk is half the reason to go.

Getting between them is cheap and rarely necessary. A single Strætó fare is 690 ISK and is valid for 75 minutes with transfers, 12- to 17-year-olds and over-67s pay 345 ISK, and under-11s travel free. There is no day pass, but there is a cap: with the same payment method you are never charged for more than three rides in a day, so 2,070 ISK is the most a day of buses can cost you.

Is the Reykjavík City Card worth it?

It is 6,100 ISK for 24 hours, 8,500 for 48 and 10,500 for 72, and it covers seventeen museums, eight pools, unlimited city buses and one Viðey ferry crossing. Under-18s get museum entry free alongside an adult card anyway.

Do the arithmetic before you buy. A pool at 1,500, the Viðey return at 2,500 and two bus rides at 690 each come to 5,380 — still short of the 6,100. The card starts winning the moment a real museum goes on the list, and it wins comfortably if you plan two.

If your plan is the walk, the tower and a pool, skip it. If your plan is a rainy day indoors, buy it.

Two of those deserve a flag. The Viðey ferry runs from the Old Harbour only between 1 June and 31 August — the rest of the year it leaves from Skarfabakki, further east, which is a bus ride rather than a stroll. And neither whale-watching operator currently publishes a sighting rate; both give you a free repeat ticket if the sea stays empty. Departure times and prices are easiest to compare across operators before you commit a daylight block to a boat.

Eating, and the one queue worth avoiding

The cheap classic is the Icelandic pylsa, a hot dog made from a lamb, pork and beef blend. Order eina með öllu, one with everything. The famous stand on Tryggvagata does not publish its price; independent surveys in 2026 put a hot dog at around 900 ISK, which still makes it the cheapest hot food in the centre by a distance.

At midday that stand has the most reliable queue in the city centre. The same chain's Austurstræti branch is open around the clock and rarely has one. In winter, spending daylight standing in a line is about the most expensive thing you can do with it.

Sit-down restaurants are genuinely expensive, and that is where a day in Reykjavik stops being cheap. Tap water is excellent and free everywhere, so a refillable bottle saves more than it sounds like it should.

What to skip, and what to do instead

  • A city sightseeing bus. The centre is twenty minutes across on foot and the bus cannot stop where the good bits are. Walk it.
  • Saving the tower for the end of the day. In winter the last lift is 16:45 and the light is gone before that. Go up early.
  • The Blue Lagoon as a city day. It is an hour out on the airport road and takes half a day from the centre. Pin it to a flight instead — the full cost and timing breakdown is a separate piece.
  • Chasing the aurora from the city centre. Street light beats it. Where to stand matters more than which night you pick.
  • Trying to fit the Golden Circle and the city into one day. It is a full day out, and you will see Reykjavik through a coach window. Give it its own day.

A shape for two days

Day one is the walk: centre, tower before the light goes, waterfront, Old Harbour, and a pool in the evening. Day two is Grandi and whatever the weather pushes you indoors for, with a lagoon slot booked for the end of it.

Where you sleep decides which pool becomes your local and how much of the walk you do twice. Anywhere inside the 101 postcode puts the whole daylight list within fifteen minutes on foot, which in December is worth more than the room. Compare central stays on that basis rather than on the star rating.

If you only have one day, the worked timetable sets it out hour by hour against the sunset. If you want the walk itself explained as you go, that is the self-guided tour. If you would rather someone talk to you in person, the tip-based walking tours have fixed departures and cover a shorter loop.

Common questions

What is the number one thing to do in Reykjavik?

Walk the centre. Everything most visitors come for sits on one line from Arnarhóll through the old streets to the Old Harbour, and that line is about 5 km and two hours. Hallgrímskirkja, the rainbow street, Harpa and the Sun Voyager are all on it, and all of them are free to stand in front of. The paid list only starts after that walk is done.

How many days do you need in Reykjavik?

Two is the honest answer for the city itself. One day covers the walk, the tower and a pool. The second buys you Grandi, a museum or two and a lagoon without rushing. Everything beyond that — the Golden Circle, the South Coast — is a day trip out of the city rather than more Reykjavik.

What can you do in Reykjavik for free?

More than the prices suggest. The whole walking route costs nothing, including the inside of Hallgrímskirkja and the inside of Harpa. The sculpture garden behind the Einar Jónsson museum holds 26 bronzes and is open year-round, day and night. The waterfront path, Tjörnin, Austurvöllur and the painted street are all free, and so is the tap water.

Is the Reykjavík City Card worth it?

Only if museums are genuinely your plan. The 24-hour card is 6,100 ISK. A pool is 1,500, the Viðey return is 2,500 and two bus rides are 1,380 — that is 5,380 before a single museum. Add the National Museum at 3,300 and it pays. Do three outdoor things and a pool instead, and it does not.

What is there to do in Reykjavik in winter when it is dark?

Most of the list, as it turns out. The pools are open until 22:00 and are better in the dark and the cold. The museums keep the same hours year-round. Perlan's planetarium show and the lagoons do not care about the sun at all. The only things the dark takes from you are the tower view and the harbour, and the aurora is the thing it gives back.

Is Reykjavik worth visiting for a few days?

Yes, and for a specific reason: it is the world's northernmost capital, and it is small enough to cross on foot in an afternoon. The geothermal water is a daily habit here rather than a spa treat, and the day trips start less than an hour from your hotel. What it is not is a city of great monuments, and a list that pretends otherwise will disappoint you.

What should you not bother with in Reykjavik?

Nothing here is a scam, but two things are usually a misallocation of hours. Queueing at the famous hot-dog stand at peak time costs more daylight than the hot dog is worth, and the Austurstræti branch is open around the clock. And a city bus tour makes little sense in a centre you can cross on foot in twenty minutes.

The short version

Rank by daylight, not by rating. Spend the bright hours on the walk, the tower and the water's edge, all of which are free except the lift. Spend the dark on pools, museums and a lagoon, which is where the money goes anyway. Two days does the city properly; one day does it honestly.
